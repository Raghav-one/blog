The history and evolution of AI

From Turing’s question to the generative era: the people, breakthroughs, disappointments, and ideas that brought us here.

R
Raghav5 October 2026 · 25 min read

It is easy to read the history of artificial intelligence backwards: start with a chatbot, look for the invention that made it possible, and draw a straight line to the present. But AI’s actual history is much more interesting. It includes competing schools of thought, machines that impressed people long before they were useful, funding that dried up, and ideas that waited decades for the right conditions.

For this first post, I want to take a historical snapshot of that journey. The technical ideas matter, but so do the moments when expectations changed: a summer meeting in 1956, an early chatbot in 1966, a chess match in 1997, a Go match in 2016, and a public chat interface in 2022. Each made people reconsider what computers could do.

1. Mathematical neurons and the Turing test 1943–1956

AI began as a question before it became a research field. Could the operations of a mind be described precisely enough for a machine to perform them?

In 1943, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts published a mathematical model of networks of simplified neurons. Their units were extremely abstract, but they connected the study of nervous systems with logic and computation. This was one early foundation for the neural-network tradition, long before the hardware or datasets of modern deep learning existed.

The McCulloch–Pitts unit produced a binary output when its inputs satisfied specified conditions. Networks of these units could implement logical operations. But the connections were specified by the designer: this was a model of computation, not yet a procedure for learning from data. That distinction explains why the later perceptron was a separate step, even though both are often grouped under the history of neural networks.

Alan Turing approached the question from another direction. In his 1950 paper, Computing Machinery and Intelligence, he replaced an argument over the definition of thinking with an imitation game: could a machine’s written replies make it difficult for an interrogator to distinguish it from a person? He also discussed learning machines. Turing’s contribution was bigger than a test; it helped make machine intelligence a serious subject for investigation.

Turing’s test concerned observable conversational behaviour, rather than measuring consciousness or inspecting the internal machinery. A symbolic program and a neural network could, in principle, both be judged through that interface. His discussion of educating a machine also anticipated a central division in the field: should intelligence be programmed directly, or developed through learning?

The field acquired its enduring name in the 1955 proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence. John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon proposed the gathering, which took place in 1956. Their ambition was striking: study whether learning and other features of intelligence could be described and simulated by machines.

Dartmouth did not deliver a thinking machine. It gave a loose collection of questions a shared identity—and a research agenda that would outlast its early optimism.

2. Symbolic AI, perceptrons, and early language systems 1956–1972

The first successes were small enough to fit inside carefully defined worlds. Allen Newell, Herbert Simon, and Cliff Shaw’s Logic Theorist, begun in 1955, demonstrated that a program could search for proofs of mathematical theorems. Symbolic AI treated reasoning as the manipulation of explicit symbols, rules, and possible steps.

For a symbolic system, a problem could be expressed as an initial state, a desired state, and legal operations connecting them. A theorem prover searched possible deductions; a planner searched possible actions. Heuristics—rules for choosing promising candidates—helped avoid checking every alternative. The strength was an explicit representation of the task. The weakness was that someone had to provide the symbols, rules, and assumptions before the search could begin.

Perceptrons and linear separability

Meanwhile, Frank Rosenblatt pursued learning rather than hand-written reasoning. His perceptron work in the late 1950s explored machines that could learn to distinguish patterns. Public attention quickly outran what these systems could actually do. The basic idea—adjust connections in response to examples—would remain influential even when enthusiasm for early neural networks weakened.

A basic perceptron takes numerical inputs, multiplies them by learned weights, adds them, and applies a threshold to choose a class. When it misclassifies an example, an update changes the weights. This turns classification into learning a decision boundary instead of listing every possible input.

The boundary of a single-layer perceptron is linear. It cannot separate every pattern: exclusive-or, or XOR, is the standard example because no single straight line separates its two classes. Minsky and Papert’s 1969 analysis made limits of perceptron approaches prominent. That was a limitation of particular architectures, not a proof that all neural networks were useless. Networks with nonlinear hidden layers can represent relationships that a single linear classifier cannot.

This is why early neural-network criticism must be read carefully. A learning rule can be effective within the functions its architecture can express while failing completely outside them. Later progress required both more expressive networks and practical ways to train their internal layers. More computation alone would not turn a single linear boundary into a nonlinear one.

ELIZA: pattern matching; Shakey: planning and robotics

In 1966, Joseph Weizenbaum published ELIZA. Its famous DOCTOR script used pattern matching and substitutions to reflect a user’s statements back as questions. ELIZA did not understand a conversation as a person would, yet the exchange could feel personal. The tension between a convincing interface and the mechanism behind it was already visible.

At SRI, Shakey, developed from 1966 to 1972, connected perception, planning, and movement. It could reason about how to complete tasks in a simplified environment, rather than needing a person to specify every movement. Shakey was slow and limited, but it showed that AI could do more than manipulate text on a screen.

These systems used different representations. ELIZA transformed text through matching rules; Shakey maintained a simplified description of objects and possible actions. A planner could ask which action sequence would satisfy a goal, while sensors and control software dealt with execution. Representation, planning, and physical control were separate problems. A successful plan still depended on the world matching the assumptions used to construct it.

These experiments made the ambition tangible. They also exposed a problem: success in a tiny, controlled world was difficult to extend to the messy world outside it.

3. Search complexity and the first AI winter 1966–1970s

Early demonstrations encouraged predictions of rapid progress. But computers had little memory and processing power, language was full of ambiguity, and searching possible solutions could become overwhelmingly expensive as a problem grew.

The technical issue was often combinatorial explosion. Imagine ten possible actions at each step and a plan six steps long: there are one million candidate sequences of that length alone. This is an illustrative search tree, not a measurement of an early AI system. It shows why an algorithm that worked on a small example could become impractical after a modest increase in problem size. Better heuristics and representations were needed alongside faster machines.

Language introduced another difficulty. Translating a sentence required more than replacing words from a dictionary: word order, ambiguity, and implied context changed the meaning. Early systems often encoded limited grammar and vocabulary explicitly. Expanding those rules increased their complexity without automatically supplying the background knowledge needed to interpret unfamiliar sentences.

The 1966 ALPAC report questioned the practical value of the machine-translation systems then available and recommended a different balance of research and support. In Britain, the 1973 Lighthill report criticised AI’s ability to scale from laboratory examples to real problems; government funding contracted sharply. Jonathan Grudin’s historical account places this episode in the broader cycle of promises, disappointing results, and redirected funding.

This became part of the period commonly called the first AI winter. The phrase can make it sound as though everyone stopped working. They did not. Different countries, laboratories, and research traditions experienced the downturn differently. What cooled most visibly was confidence that general intelligence was just around the corner.

The lesson was uncomfortable: a program that solved a puzzle did not automatically know how to navigate a room, understand a sentence, or deal with missing information. Each new setting demanded more than the demonstration had revealed.

4. Knowledge representation and expert systems 1970s–1990s

One response was to narrow the ambition. Instead of trying to reproduce intelligence in general, researchers tried to capture expertise in a specific field.

DENDRAL, begun at Stanford in the 1960s, used chemical knowledge to help interpret molecular evidence. MYCIN, developed in the 1970s, explored rule-based recommendations concerning bacterial infections. These were expert systems: a knowledge base plus an inference process for applying it. The Computer History Museum’s discussion with expert-system pioneers describes how the amount and quality of specialist knowledge became central to performance.

Inference rules, backward chaining, and uncertainty

An expert system separated the knowledge base from the inference engine. The knowledge base held facts and rules; the engine determined which rules could apply. Forward chaining starts with known facts and derives consequences. Backward chaining starts with a proposed conclusion and works backwards to find the evidence needed to establish it. The separation made it possible to revise domain knowledge without rewriting the entire reasoning engine.

MYCIN used backward chaining and certainty factors to reason with uncertain evidence. A conclusion could have graded support rather than being simply true or false. These certainty factors were a particular formalism for combining confidence; they should not be read as universally calibrated probabilities. The system’s explanation facility could also expose which rules contributed to a recommendation.

Forward and backward chaining: a rule example

Here is a small illustrative rule system, not a reconstruction of MYCIN. Its known facts are temperature_high and fan_stopped. Its rules are temperature_high AND fan_stopped → cooling_fault and cooling_fault → inspect_fan.

Forward chaining begins with the two observations. The first rule adds cooling_fault to the known facts, which enables the second rule to recommend inspect_fan. Backward chaining begins with the question “should we inspect the fan?” It looks for a rule that establishes that conclusion, discovers the required condition cooling_fault, and then checks the observations needed to establish it.

The two directions use the same rules but visit the reasoning space differently. Forward chaining suits a stream of facts from which many consequences may follow. Backward chaining can focus work on a particular question. Neither invents missing rules: if a new failure mode was never represented, the inference engine cannot recover the omitted expertise simply by searching harder. This makes the historical maintenance problem more concrete.

During the 1980s, this approach attracted business investment. The appeal was straightforward: a computer could apply scarce expertise consistently within a defined domain. But gathering that knowledge, resolving conflicting rules, and keeping a system current required substantial human work. Expertise did not turn into a maintainable program automatically.

This architecture created a knowledge-acquisition bottleneck. Experts had to articulate distinctions they often used implicitly, and engineers had to translate them into rules. Adding a new case could require checking its interaction with existing rules. The resulting system might handle a specialist task well while lacking the common sense needed to recognise that an unfamiliar case fell outside its competence.

By the late 1980s and early 1990s, enthusiasm for expert systems and specialised AI hardware had cooled. This is usually described as a second winter. Yet researchers continued developing other approaches. In a later oral-history discussion, Edward Feigenbaum challenged the blanket winter narrative: work was often flourishing elsewhere while one approach lost attention.

Research continued through the downturn, with increasing emphasis on statistical methods and learning from data alongside established symbolic approaches.

5. Backpropagation and statistical machine learning 1986–2000s

Neural networks had not disappeared. David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams’ 1986 paper on backpropagation helped popularise a way to train networks with internal layers. In plain terms, a network makes an error, and the training procedure works backwards to determine how its connections should change. The method had earlier roots; 1986 was an influential milestone, not a creation from nothing.

Loss functions, gradients, and hidden representations

A multilayer neural network transforms an input through successive layers. A loss function measures the difference between its prediction and the desired result. Backpropagation uses the chain rule to calculate how each parameter influences that loss. An optimiser then changes the parameters to reduce it. Backpropagation computes gradients; it is not itself the whole optimisation algorithm.

Nonlinear hidden layers matter because they allow the network to learn intermediate representations. Without nonlinearities, stacking ordinary linear transformations still produces a linear transformation. During training, useful internal features can emerge even when the dataset specifies only the final target. This addressed a different problem from symbolic inference: fitting a model from examples rather than proving a conclusion from rules.

Gradient descent: one numerical update

A minimal example separates the prediction, the loss, and the update. Let the model be ŷ = w × x, with input x = 2, target y = 6, and initial weight w = 0. Its prediction is 0. Using L = ½(ŷ − y)², the initial loss is 18.

The derivative is dL/dw = (ŷ − y) × x = −12. With learning rate 0.1, gradient descent updates the weight to w − 0.1 × (−12) = 1.2. The new prediction is 2.4 and the new loss is 6.48. The update improved this example; it did not solve it in one step.

In a multilayer network, backpropagation carries this derivative calculation through all the intermediate operations. Training repeats updates across many examples. Performance on those examples is only part of the objective: a model can learn the training set closely and still generalise poorly. Held-out evaluation therefore became essential to the statistical-learning approach. The historical change was not merely “more layers,” but a workable process for adjusting those layers and measuring their behaviour on unseen data.

Support vector machines and recurrent neural networks

Over the following years, statistical learning grew in importance. Instead of describing every rule explicitly, researchers measured how well models predicted patterns in data. Speech recognition, handwriting recognition, and other practical tasks provided settings where progress could be evaluated without settling the philosophical question of whether a machine “thought.”

Statistical machine learning also included methods that were not deep neural networks. Corinna Cortes and Vladimir Vapnik’s 1995 support-vector network paper developed a classifier based on separating classes with a margin while allowing some errors. Kernel methods made nonlinear separation possible through comparisons in an implicit feature space. These methods strengthened a practical pattern: choose a representation, fit a model, and evaluate it on data it did not see during training.

For sequences, recurrent neural networks reused a state that was updated as each element arrived. The state carried information from earlier steps, but training across long sequences could suffer from vanishing or exploding gradients. Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber’s 1997 long short-term memory, or LSTM, work introduced a memory mechanism and gates for controlling information flow. This helped networks learn dependencies across time and became influential in speech and language processing before transformers.

Deep Blue: chess search and evaluation (1997)

In May 1997, IBM’s Deep Blue defeated reigning world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match. Chess had long served as a symbol of intellectual skill, so the result carried enormous cultural weight.

Deep Blue combined specialised hardware, extensive search, and chess-specific evaluation. It was not a general learner that could transfer its victory to any other task. But it showed that a computer could outperform the strongest human in a domain once treated as a test of intelligence. The historical significance lay in both the achievement and the boundary: exceptional performance at chess was possible without a general-purpose mind.

The field was moving toward systems that could be judged by what they accomplished. Growing computing resources and larger digital datasets would make that shift increasingly powerful.

6. Convolutional neural networks and reinforcement learning 2012–2016

Yann LeCun’s work on handwritten character recognition had already demonstrated the importance of local connections and shared weights before the 2012 ImageNet result. In a convolutional neural network (CNN), a learned filter is applied across different parts of an input. The same feature detector can therefore recognise a pattern at more than one location, without learning a separate set of parameters for every position.

In 2012, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton’s AlexNet achieved a striking result in the ImageNet image-classification competition. A deep convolutional neural network, trained using GPUs, substantially outperformed the other entries.

The ingredients were not all new. Neural networks had a long history; convolutional networks predated AlexNet. What changed was the combination of a large labelled dataset, enough computation, and techniques that made a bigger network effective. The result made deep learning difficult for the wider research community to ignore.

The technical shift was that useful visual representations could be learned through layers, rather than relying entirely on features designed by people. It accelerated a period in which neural methods spread through computer vision and other fields.

Layers combine local responses into more complex representations. Later units can depend on a wider part of the image, while the final classifier uses the resulting representation to predict a category. Training adjusts both the feature extractors and the classifier. Historically, this reduced the need to design every useful visual feature by hand; it did not remove the need to choose training data, architecture, and evaluation criteria.

AlphaGo: policy networks, value networks, and search (2016)

In March 2016, Google DeepMind’s AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol 4–1. Go’s vast search space made the achievement feel different from computer chess. AlphaGo combined neural networks, search, and reinforcement learning: learning from outcomes to improve decisions.

The AlphaGo architecture combined a policy network, which favoured promising moves, with a value network, which estimated how favourable a position was. Monte Carlo tree search used those predictions to explore possible continuations. Learning reduced the effective search problem; search then improved the decision beyond simply selecting the network’s first suggestion.

Reinforcement learning differs from ordinary supervised classification. The learner chooses actions, observes outcomes, and seeks a higher expected reward. In a game, the final win or loss can be far removed from the move that contributed to it: this is a credit-assignment problem. Self-play provided a way to generate experience and improve strategies without asking people to label every decision.

Its moves became a subject of fascination because some looked unfamiliar to expert players. Here was a system that could surprise people within a game they knew intimately. A year later, AlphaGo Zero demonstrated a version that learned through self-play without human game examples.

Deep Blue and AlphaGo are often grouped together as machines beating champions. Historically, the distinction matters: they showcased different combinations of computation, domain knowledge, and learning. Neither victory, by itself, established general intelligence.

7. Transformers and pretrained language models 2017–2020

The next turning point arrived as a research paper rather than a televised contest. In 2017, Ashish Vaswani and colleagues introduced the transformer in Attention Is All You Need.

The original task was machine translation. Attention allowed the model to weigh relationships between elements of a sequence, while the architecture avoided the step-by-step recurrence used by earlier sequence models. This made more parallel training possible. It was not the invention of attention, but it established an architecture that would become central to modern language modelling.

Self-attention and encoder–decoder architectures

In self-attention, each token representation produces a query, key, and value. Query–key comparisons determine which other tokens receive more weight; a weighted combination of their values produces a context-sensitive representation. Multiple attention heads learn different combinations. Position information supplies sequence order, while feed-forward layers and residual connections contribute further transformations. Harvard’s annotated implementation makes these components explicit.

The original transformer had an encoder to represent the input sentence and a decoder to generate its translation. The decoder attended to the encoder’s output and used a causal mask to prevent access to future output tokens. Parallel computation helped training, but generating an autoregressive answer still typically proceeds token by token. Parallel training does not mean that an entire generated response appears in one independent step.

Attention: a weighted combination of information

For one attention head, suppose a token assigns weights 0.25 and 0.75 to two other positions. If their value vectors are [2, 0] and [0, 4], the combined vector is 0.25 × [2, 0] + 0.75 × [0, 4] = [0.5, 3]. These are illustrative numbers, not measurements from a trained language model.

The important operation is content-dependent routing: different queries produce different weights over available information. In scaled dot-product attention, query–key scores are scaled and passed through a softmax to obtain those weights. A causal mask rules out future positions for a decoder. The model can then build a representation of a word that depends on the surrounding sentence, rather than using one fixed interpretation everywhere.

This addresses a bottleneck of recurrent processing. A recurrent network repeatedly updates a carried state; attention gives a position a more direct route to other positions in the current context. It has a cost: ordinary full self-attention compares pairs of positions, so its attention matrix grows quadratically with sequence length. The transformer improved important aspects of language modelling without making context length or memory free.

BERT, GPT, and language-model pretraining

Models such as BERT and GPT then helped popularise a different development pattern: pretrain on broad text, then adapt the resulting model to particular tasks. Instead of starting from an empty model each time, researchers could reuse capabilities acquired during large-scale training.

BERT, published in 2018, used an encoder with a masked-language objective: infer hidden tokens using the surrounding text. Its representations could be fine-tuned for classification, question answering, and other tasks. GPT-style models used a causal language objective: predict the next token from earlier tokens. That objective naturally supports continuation and generation. These are related architectures with different information boundaries during training.

The 2020 GPT-3 paper made another possibility especially visible. A large language model could perform many tasks from instructions or examples placed in its prompt, without updating its weights separately for each task. The prompt was becoming a way to specify what the model should do.

This also introduced an important distinction between fine-tuning and in-context learning. Fine-tuning updates model weights using additional training examples. In-context learning puts examples in the input while leaving the weights unchanged during the interaction. Both can alter behaviour, but the information is stored differently: in parameters in one case, and temporarily in the prompt in the other.

Underneath, these models learned to predict pieces of text from context. At sufficient scale, this training supported a much wider range of behaviour than a single-purpose text predictor might suggest. It also made the relationship between data, computing budgets, and capability central to the frontier.

8. Foundation models, diffusion, and instruction tuning 2020–2023

The broader story was not confined to language. AlphaFold’s 2020 performance at CASP14 marked a major advance in protein-structure prediction. It showed another route to impact: an AI system could contribute to scientific research, beyond games and consumer applications. Predicting a structure still does not solve every question about a protein’s behaviour.

Foundation models and diffusion-based generation

The term foundation model, formalised in a 2021 Stanford report, describes a model trained on broad data that can support multiple downstream uses. It is a description of its role, not a requirement that it use a particular architecture. A pretrained model still needs the right adaptation, interface, or surrounding system for a specific application.

Generative systems also brought images and text to wider public attention. Instead of returning only a category or score, they produced new content from a user’s description. For many people, the visible change was that AI became something they could direct themselves.

Image generation followed another influential route. Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain, and Pieter Abbeel’s 2020 denoising diffusion work trained a network to reverse a gradual corruption process. During training, noise is added to an image and the model learns a denoising task. During generation, a sample starts from noise and is refined over multiple steps. Text conditioning can guide the result. This is a different generation mechanism from predicting the next text token.

Diffusion training and sampling

The forward corruption process is specified in advance: take a real training image and add a controlled amount of noise. A common training formulation gives the network the noisy image and the noise level, and asks it to predict the noise that was added. The training target is therefore available without a person drawing a cleaned image for every noisy input.

Sampling reverses the direction. Starting from noise, the sampler uses the learned prediction to make successive denoising updates. Training learns the denoising model; sampling uses it to construct a new image. A text-conditioned version adds a representation of the requested description to guide those updates. The output is a sample from the learned generative process, not necessarily a retrieved image. But successful generation alone does not establish originality, faithful prompt following, or the absence of memorised training material.

Supervised instruction tuning and RLHF

On 30 November 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT as a research preview. The conversational interface made language-model capabilities easy to try: ask a question, request a draft, follow up, or revise an answer. Instruction training and reinforcement learning from human feedback helped shape the model into a more usable assistant.

Pretraining teaches a language model to continue text, but that objective does not explicitly require it to answer a user’s request. The 2022 InstructGPT work described a pipeline of human demonstrations, supervised fine-tuning, human rankings of candidate answers, and reinforcement learning using a learned reward model. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) teaches response patterns from examples; reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) uses preferences to shape which responses become more likely.

These stages changed the relationship between the base model and its users. A model trained for continuation could be adapted into an assistant that more consistently followed instructions. But a preference reward is not a proof of factual accuracy. A helpful-looking response can score well while containing an error, especially when the evaluator cannot easily verify the claim.

ChatGPT was not the beginning of AI, nor the first chatbot. Its historical importance was that it put a broadly capable language model behind an accessible interface. Decades of research became visible to people who had never read a machine-learning paper.

That visibility exposed the limitations too. The launch itself warned that the system could produce plausible but incorrect answers and could be sensitive to how a prompt was phrased. A fluent response could feel authoritative even when its content was wrong. The old ELIZA question—what do we infer from a convincing conversation?—had returned at a very different scale.

9. Reasoning models and tool-using agents 2024 onward

More recent work has expanded the question from what a model can say to how it can tackle a difficult task. In 2024, OpenAI’s o1 work demonstrated that training for extended reasoning, combined with more computation before answering, could improve performance on evaluated reasoning problems. It added a dimension to scaling: computation at use time, as well as during training.

Here, inference-time computation means spending resources while solving a particular input: generating intermediate reasoning, considering alternatives, or checking a proposed answer. It differs from pretraining, which changes the model through a large learning process before deployment. More inference computation can improve some tasks while increasing latency and cost; the benefit depends on whether the extra computation is directed productively.

The DeepSeek-R1 work published in 2025 explored reinforcement learning for reasoning and released model weights, widening opportunities to study and build on this approach. Neither a longer reasoning process nor an impressive benchmark guarantees that every answer will be correct.

Reasoning training can also use rewards tied to an externally checkable result, such as a mathematical answer or a program passing tests. This is often called reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. Its advantage is that a checker can supply a more concrete signal than a general preference judgement. Its limit is the checker: passing an incomplete test suite or satisfying a narrow answer format is not the same as solving the intended problem in every setting.

Verifiable rewards and benchmark boundaries

Consider a mathematical training task whose answer can be checked exactly. The system generates a solution, an evaluator extracts its final answer, and a reward is assigned according to whether that answer is correct. Reinforcement learning changes the probability of generated behaviours using this signal. A correct final answer can supply a useful training signal even when nobody labels every intermediate step.

For code, the evaluator might execute tests. That makes the reward more operational, but also ties it to test coverage. A program that returns the expected output for the supplied cases may still fail on an omitted input. The reward defines what is encouraged; the evaluation defines what has actually been demonstrated. This is why an improvement on a checked benchmark must be distinguished from a guarantee of general reasoning ability.

Tool use, retrieval, and agent control loops

In parallel, models have been connected to search, code execution, and other tools. Anthropic’s 2024 account of effective agents distinguishes predefined workflows from systems where a model dynamically directs the process. The model is one component; the surrounding system supplies tools, context, feedback, and boundaries.

Tool use changes how a system obtains information. A model can request a search or a calculation; software executes the request and returns an observation for the next model step. ReAct, introduced in 2022, explored interleaving reasoning and actions in this way. The tool result supplies evidence that was not necessarily present in the model’s weights. This is also why tool use and reasoning-model training are separate developments that can be combined.

An agent control loop repeats the sequence of interpreting the current state, choosing an action, observing its result, and deciding whether to continue. A workflow can prescribe that sequence in advance; an agent can let the model choose parts of it dynamically. The additional flexibility introduces operational requirements: limit permissions, preserve relevant state, validate tool outputs, and define when the system should stop. These requirements become more consequential as the system moves from describing an action to executing it.

There is a historical echo here. Shakey already combined perception, planning, and action in a limited world. Modern language-based agents use different machinery and operate in different environments, but the ambition to turn a goal into actions is older than the latest model release.

For me, that is what makes this history useful. The frontier has moved from tightly defined demonstrations toward systems used in ordinary work, yet recurring questions remain: how far does a result generalise, what assumptions make it possible, and where does it break?

AI’s evolution is a story of accumulated ideas, changing resources, and changing expectations. New approaches have expanded the field without erasing everything that came before. Understanding that history makes the present more interesting—and its promises easier to judge.

A note on scope

This is a selective historical overview, not a complete inventory of AI. Era boundaries overlap, “AI winter” is an uneven label, and recent examples mark documented shifts rather than a ranking of today’s models.
