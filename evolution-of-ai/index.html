01 / FOUNDATIONS

The evolution
of AI.

From writing the rules to teaching machines to learn, generate, and act. A short history of the ideas that moved the frontier.

RRaghav
5 October 2026 · 5 min read

The most useful way to understand AI’s evolution is to ask one question: who supplies the method for solving the problem? At first, people wrote it down. Then machines learned it from examples. Today, broadly trained models can generate responses and, inside larger systems, use tools to work toward a goal.

Consider one task throughout this story: helping a customer find a missing delivery. The task stays the same. What changes is how the system handles it.

01 THE STARTING POINT

When intelligence meant rules

The 1955 Dartmouth proposal gave the field its name and proposed a summer research project for 1956.[1] Much early AI pursued symbolic reasoning: represent facts explicitly, then manipulate them with logic and search.

A delivery assistant might follow a rule: if overdue and not delivered → escalate. This is inspectable and useful when the world is predictable. But someone must anticipate the exceptions. A sarcastic message, an unfamiliar address, or a new carrier can expose a gap in the rulebook.

The bottleneck: human effort spent describing the world.

02 THE FUNDAMENTAL SHIFT

From instructions to examples

Machine learning changes the bargain. Give a model examples and a measurable objective; training adjusts its internal parameters to reduce error. A classifier can learn which messages signal a late delivery without an engineer writing every phrase it might encounter.

Deep learning extends this idea through layers that learn representations. In 2012, AlexNet demonstrated a major image-classification improvement using a deep neural network trained on GPUs.[2] It was a landmark in the resurgence of deep learning, not the invention of neural networks.

THE TRAINING LOOP
Examples→Prediction→Measure error→Adjust weights

Repeat during training. At use time, the learned weights produce a prediction.

The tradeoff moves too. We now need representative data and good evaluation. A model that recognizes yesterday’s complaints can still fail on a new kind of problem.

The bottleneck: the quality of the examples and objective.

03 THE GENERAL-PURPOSE TURN

One model, many possible tasks

The transformer, introduced in 2017, made attention central to a new sequence-model architecture.[3] Attention lets a model weigh relationships between tokens—pieces of text—rather than treating each word as an isolated item. Its design also enabled more parallel training than recurrent approaches.

Large language models trained on broad text learn to predict tokens from context. The 2020 GPT-3 paper showed that a single large model could perform many tasks from instructions and examples in a prompt, without separate weight updates for each task.[4] Later instruction tuning and preference-based training helped turn this capability into more usable assistants.

Our delivery assistant can now interpret a messy complaint, summarize its meaning, and draft a helpful reply. But generating a convincing answer does not give it access to the parcel’s real location. Fluency is not a live database.

THE DISTINCTION THAT MATTERS

A model can learn how a helpful answer sounds.
It still needs evidence that the answer is true.

04 TOWARD THE FRONTIER

Reasoning time, tools, and feedback

Frontier AI refers here to models near the leading edge of capability; it is a moving boundary, not a single architecture. Two especially useful developments are models trained for extended reasoning and systems that connect models to tools.

OpenAI’s 2024 o1 work showed that reinforcement learning and more computation at inference time could improve performance on evaluated reasoning tasks.[5] Spending more time before an answer can help, but it does not guarantee correctness.

Tools solve a different problem: contact with the outside world. A delivery system can query tracking data, inspect the response, and choose its next step. Anthropic distinguishes workflows with predefined paths from agents that dynamically direct their own processes and tool use.[6] An agent is a system around a model, not simply a more powerful chatbot.

OUR DELIVERY EXAMPLE
  1. 01
    UnderstandIdentify the order and the customer’s concern.
  2. 02
    CheckQuery the tracking tool for current evidence.
  3. 03
    Respond or actExplain the status; request approval before a refund.
  4. 04
    VerifyCheck the result, then stop or escalate.

Every extra action adds opportunities for error. Keep permissions narrow, record tool results, and test the full workflow. A wrong sentence and a wrong refund have different consequences.

WHAT TO WATCH NEXT

The frontier is also a reliability problem.

My reading of this history is that each shift expands what can be attempted while changing what must be checked. Rules need coverage. Learned models need representative tests. Generated answers need evidence. Agents need controls and verified outcomes.

When the next AI announcement arrives, ask: what can it do, what evidence proves it, and what happens when it is wrong? That tells you more than a claim that it is “smarter.”
